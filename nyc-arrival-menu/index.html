New York’s Arrival Menu The Oyster City

Four centuries · Five boroughs · Read in order

Nothing on this menu is from here.

New York’s signature dishes are a four-hundred-year record of arrivals: a Romanian cure, a Neapolitan oven, a Polish boiled roll, an Egyptian hot-dog cart that changed its mind. Only the oyster was already here — and that was Lenape food for centuries before anyone thought to call it a New York dish. What follows is the city’s menu in chronological order, which turns out to be the same thing as its immigration record.

1609to1860

The Oyster City

Before the restaurant and long before the sandwich, the harbour fed the city for almost nothing. This is the only era on the menu whose defining dish was already growing in the water when the first ship came in.

Arriving
Lenape (Munsee) foodways ◆ Dutch ◆ English ◆ Irish ◆ Germans
1825
The Erie Canal opens. New York becomes the country’s port of entry for both grain and people, and every wave after this one lands here first.

1609to 1927

The Oyster

When Henry Hudson’s crew came into the harbour in 1609, something in the order of 220,000 acres of oyster beds lay under it — by the estimate that gets repeated most often, close to half the world’s supply. The Lenape had been harvesting them for centuries. Their shell middens ran block-long along the shore, and two of the islands in the harbour were named for the trade: Little Oyster Island is now Ellis Island, Great Oyster Island is now Liberty Island.

For most of the nineteenth century the oyster was not a luxury but the cheapest protein in town. Cellar saloons, marked from the street by a red gas globe, sold the Canal Street Plan — all you could eat for six cents, with the understanding that a hostile barman would hand you a bad one if you pushed the arrangement too far. Canal Street itself was paved with crushed shell. Carts sold them the way carts sell pretzels now.

Then the city caught up with its own harbour. Sewage outfalls, typhoid traced back to Jamaica Bay, bed after bed condemned; the last commercial beds closed in 1927. The oyster came back as a luxury, imported, on ice. The Billion Oyster Project has since put more than 150 million back in the water — not to eat, but to filter.

Arrived with
Nobody. Lenape food first, then the Dutch and Irish cellar trade.
Peak
The 1880s, when the city ate them by the hundreds of millions a year.
Eat it at
Grand Central Oyster Bar, lower level, Grand Central Terminal.
The rule
Ask what came in this morning, then order that and nothing else.

In the margins

Delmonico’s invents the restaurant

1827 · 1837

Two Swiss brothers, Giovanni and Pietro, opened a café on William Street in 1827 and a full restaurant by 1837 — the first place in America to hand you a menu and let you order à la carte, eleven pages of it, in French and English. The Delmonico steak, Lobster Newberg and Baked Alaska are all claimed here. So are Eggs Benedict, which the Waldorf also claims: the first of many New York food arguments with no referee.

The shilling oyster stand

c. 1840

Thomas Downing, a free Black oysterman from Virginia, ran the most fashionable oyster house in the city on Broad Street — bankers and visiting aristocrats upstairs, and, by most accounts, a stop on the Underground Railroad in the cellar below. His son George T. Downing carried the politics further, campaigning for desegregated schools and Black voting rights. It is the earliest example of a pattern this whole page keeps repeating.

1860to1900

The Gilded Table

Two cities eating at once. Oyster forks and eleven-page menus on one side of town; a sausage in a split roll, eaten standing on the sand at Coney Island, on the other. The second one is the tradition that lasted.

Arriving
Germans of Kleindeutschland ◆ Irish ◆ from 1881, Eastern European Jews ◆ from 1880, Southern Italians
1882
The Chinese Exclusion Act — the first US law to bar a nationality by name. It shapes what Chinatown’s kitchens become, and the consequences show up on this menu ninety years later.

1867Coney Island

The Coney Island Hot Dog

Charles Feltman sold pies off a wagon to the beer gardens along the Coney Island beach. The story — and it is a story, repeated so often it has hardened into a fact — is that in 1867 he had a wheelwright fit the cart with a tin box and a charcoal burner so he could serve a hot sausage in a split milk roll: hot food, no plate, no fork, eat it walking. By 1900 the cart had become a nine-acre resort.

In 1916 one of his employees, a Polish immigrant named Nathan Handwerker, opened a stand across the way and sold the same sandwich for a nickel against Feltman’s dime. Feltman’s closed in 1954. Nathan’s is still on the corner.

What is actually certain here is smaller than the legend and more interesting than it. Germans had been eating sausage in bread for a very long time. Coney Island is where it stopped being German and became public — something a million people ate standing up, at the beach, in a city teaching itself to eat in the street.

Arrived with
German butchers of Kleindeutschland, on the Lower East Side.
First stand
Feltman’s, 1867 — the date is traditional and the claim is disputed.
Eat it at
Nathan’s Famous, Surf & Stillwell — or any cart with a rolling steam box.
The rule
Mustard, and kraut if it is there. Ketchup is a decision you are making about yourself.

1888Ludlow Street

Pastrami on Rye

Pastrami is a Romanian word — pastramă — for a Turkish technique, pastırma, applied in New York to a cut nobody in Romania was bothering with: the navel, the fatty belly end of the brisket. Cure it hard in salt and spice, smoke it, then steam it for hours until the fat finally gives up.

Sussman Volk, a Lithuanian butcher on Delancey Street, is usually credited with the first New York pastrami sandwich in the 1880s. The recipe, the story goes, was payment from a Romanian friend whose trunk he had agreed to store. In 1888 the Iceland brothers opened a delicatessen on Ludlow Street that eventually became Katz’s.

The sandwich has exactly one rule, and the entire city enforces it: rye, mustard, nothing else. A deli willing to put lettuce and tomato on pastrami is a deli willing to do other things too.

Arrived with
Romanian and Lithuanian Jewish butchers.
First served
1880s, Delancey Street — credited to Sussman Volk, disputed like everything else.
Eat it at
Katz’s (1888), 2nd Ave Deli, Sarge’s. Take the ticket. Do not lose the ticket.
The rule
Hand-carved, never machine-sliced. Tip the carver and ask for it fatty.

In the margins

The porterhouse at Peter Luger

1887

Opened in Williamsburg as Carl Luger’s Café, Billiards and Bowling Alley, sixteen years before there was a Williamsburg Bridge to sit under. Dry-aged porterhouse, broiled hard, carved off the bone and served on a tilted plate so the fat runs down to meet the bread. Sol Forman, a neighbour who had eaten lunch there most days for years, bought the place at auction in 1950 when nobody else bid.

The Waldorf salad

1896

Oscar Tschirky — “Oscar of the Waldorf,” who was the maître d’hôtel and never technically a chef — put apples, celery and mayonnaise in a bowl for a charity ball. The walnuts everyone assumes are essential were somebody else’s idea, added later. A city inventing a luxury vocabulary from three cheap ingredients is very much the point.

1900to1930

The Tenement Kitchen

Roughly two million Eastern European Jews and four million Italians arrive in a single generation, into a Lower East Side more densely packed than anywhere else on earth. Nearly everything you think of as New York food dates from these thirty years.

Arriving
Eastern European Jews ◆ Southern Italians ◆ Greeks ◆ Poles ◆ Ukrainians ◆ Syrians and Lebanese
1924
The Johnson–Reed Act sets national-origin quotas and effectively shuts the door for forty-one years. The arrivals stop. The food stays, and spends four decades becoming “American.”

1905then 1945

The Slice

Gennaro Lombardi’s grocery on Spring Street is traditionally given as America’s first licensed pizzeria, in 1905. The date is traditional, the paperwork is thin, and Neapolitan bakers were almost certainly selling pizza in the neighbourhood before anyone got round to licensing it. Those first pies came out of coal ovens built for bread — blistered, charred, sold whole.

The slice is a later and far more New York invention. After the Second World War, gas deck ovens made it possible to hold a pie hot and sell it by the piece, and a city that ate on the sidewalk did the rest. Wide, thin, foldable along the spine, sized to one hand and eaten standing over a paper plate: a pizza engineered around a lunch break.

Brooklyn keeps the rival tradition. The L&B Spumoni Gardens square — focaccia-thick, cheese under the sauce — is what people from Bensonhurst will tell you the real one is, and they are not entirely wrong.

Arrived with
Neapolitan bakers, from the 1880s onward.
First licensed
Lombardi’s, 1905 — the traditional date, not a documented one.
Eat it at
Joe’s on Carmine for the plain slice; L&B Spumoni Gardens for the square; Totonno’s (1924) for coal.
The rule
Fold it. A knife and fork on a slice is a tell, and everyone in the shop notices.

1907Local 338

Bagel, Lox, Schmear

The beigel came with Polish Jewish bakers; the earliest known written mention of it is a 1610 community ordinance in Kraków. What New York added was industrial discipline. Bagel Bakers Local 338, organised in 1907, held the trade to a few hundred men — hand-rolled, boiled in a kettle, baked on burlap-covered boards, apprenticeships handed down father to son. If you wanted a bagel in this city, you went through the union.

Lox is Yiddish laks: salt-cured salmon belly, brined hard for the barrel, a Pacific fish arriving by rail at a Lower East Side counter. Cream cheese came from upstate dairies. None of the three is a tradition anyone imported. They are what happened when a Polish bread, a Scandinavian cure and an American dairy accident ended up on the same block.

Then Lender’s put bagels in the freezer aisle in the 1960s, the union’s grip broke, and the bagel went national — bigger, softer, steamed instead of boiled. What you are looking for is a small one with a blister on it.

Arrived with
Polish and Russian Jewish bakers.
Organised
Bagel Bakers Local 338, 1907 — roughly 300 men held the whole city’s supply.
Eat it at
Russ & Daughters for the appetising counter; Absolute or Ess-a-Bagel for the bagel itself.
The rule
Belly lox is salt-cured and aggressive. Nova is cold-smoked and mild. Know which one you asked for.

c. 1900Lower East Side

The Egg Cream

No egg. No cream. Milk, seltzer, and Fox’s U-Bet chocolate syrup, which has been made in Brooklyn since 1900 and is not optional. Louis Auster’s candy store on the Lower East Side is the claim that gets repeated most; the etymology is a genuine open question, the two leading guesses being Yiddish echt (“genuine”) cream and a worn-down “chocolate cream.”

The construction is the entire drink. Syrup into the bottom of a tall glass, cold milk on top of it, then seltzer fired hard off the back of a spoon so it whips a white collar onto the surface without stirring the syrup in. You do the stirring yourself. It has to be made in front of you and drunk inside about a minute, which is why it never survived being bottled and why it now lives almost entirely in luncheonettes that never got around to replacing the counter.

Arrived with
Jewish candy-store soda fountains, c. 1890s–1900s.
Eat it at
Ray’s Candy Store on Avenue A; the counter at S&P Lunch, in the old Eisenberg’s.
The rule
Three ingredients and one of them is a brand. No U-Bet, no egg cream.

In the margins

Manhattan clam chowder

c. 1890s

Tomatoes in chowder, most likely by way of Portuguese and Italian fishermen working the same waters. The name was applied by outsiders and was meant as an insult. In 1939 a Maine legislator introduced a bill to make putting tomatoes in clam chowder a punishable offence. It did not pass, but the fact that it was filed at all tells you how the rest of the coast felt about this city.

The knish

1890 · 1910

Yonah Schimmel started with a pushcart on the Lower East Side around 1890 and had a storefront on East Houston by 1910. Baked, not fried; potato or kasha; the dough rolled out thin as a bedsheet and wrapped round the filling like a parcel. Still there, still slightly damp in the middle, still correct.

The seltzer man

1900s–now

At its height the city had thousands of men delivering pressurised siphon bottles in wooden crates to apartment doors. Seltzer was tenement medicine and tenement luxury at once — free fizz for people who could not afford a soda fountain. Two or three delivery routes still survive, running the same refillable blue glass bottles, some of them a century old.

The pushcart ban

1938

Mayor La Guardia spent the 1930s clearing tens of thousands of pushcarts off the streets and into enclosed municipal markets, on grounds of traffic and hygiene. The Essex Street Market survives from that programme. It is worth remembering the next time someone calls the halal cart on the corner an eyesore: this city has had the same argument with its own street food roughly once a generation.

1930to1965

The American Century

The door is shut. What arrives now comes from inside the country instead — six million Black Southerners heading north, and Puerto Ricans moving as citizens — while the food already here hardens into an industry, a brand and a joke on television.

Arriving
Black Southerners of the Great Migration ◆ Puerto Ricans ◆ Chinese families under exclusion ◆ postwar Holocaust survivors
1917
The Jones Act makes Puerto Ricans US citizens. The great migration to East Harlem follows after 1945 — no quota applies, because legally this is not immigration at all.

1929then 1950

New York Cheesecake

The technical break with every European cheesecake is cream cheese, which was an accident: a dairyman in Chester, New York, in 1872, trying to copy Neufchâtel and overshooting badly on fat. New York bakers took the accident and built a dessert on top of it — no curd, no ricotta, no crust worth the name, just cheese, eggs, sugar and cream baked dense enough to cut clean and served cold enough to put up a fight.

Arnold Reuben claimed he brought the recipe to his restaurant in the 1920s. Lindy’s sold it to Broadway and the columnists made it famous. Then in 1950 Harry Rosen opened Junior’s on Flatbush Avenue Extension and slid a thin layer of sponge cake underneath instead of a graham crust, which the rest of the city has been objecting to ever since without ever quite stopping eating it.

Arrived with
German-Jewish bakers, on top of an upstate dairy mistake.
Eat it at
Junior’s, Flatbush & DeKalb; Veniero’s (1894) if you want the Italian argument instead.
The rule
Plain. Strawberry topping is for people who do not like cheesecake.

1938Harlem

Chicken and Waffles

Wells opened on Seventh Avenue in 1938 and kept hours that matched its neighbourhood. Musicians came off the bandstand at two or three in the morning, too late to be served dinner and too early for breakfast, so Wells put both on one plate. Chicken and waffles is a Harlem solution to a Harlem schedule, and it stayed a night dish for decades before anyone thought to serve it at noon.

The larger kitchen it belongs to came north with the Great Migration: roughly six million Black Southerners left the South between 1916 and 1970, and a few hundred thousand of them landed in Harlem and Bedford-Stuyvesant with Carolina rice cookery, Virginia ham curing and Louisiana seasoning intact. Sylvia Woods, from Hemingway, South Carolina, opened a thirty-five-seat luncheonette on Lenox Avenue in 1962 and stayed there for fifty years.

Arrived with
The Great Migration, 1916–1970.
First served
Wells Supper Club, 1938 — as a night dish, not a brunch one.
Eat it at
Sylvia’s (1962), Charles Pan-Fried Chicken, Amy Ruth’s.
The rule
The waffle is a plate, not a dessert. Syrup and hot sauce are not in conflict.

1970svia Taipei

General Tso’s Chicken

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred labourers but left a gap for merchants, and restaurant ownership qualified. A community legally shut out of most other work built an economy out of kitchens and laundries. New York’s Chinese restaurant scene is, in a real sense, a legal artifact.

General Tso’s chicken is neither old nor Hunanese in any form its inventor would recognise. Peng Chang-kuei, who had cooked banquets for the Nationalist government and left for Taiwan in 1949, put a sharp, salty, vinegar-forward chicken dish on a menu in Taipei in the 1950s and named it after a nineteenth-century Hunanese general. He opened a restaurant in Midtown Manhattan in the 1970s. New York tasted the dish and wanted it sweeter; it got sweeter, then it got battered, and then it got away from him entirely. He lived to watch it become the most famous Chinese dish in America and was reportedly unimpressed by what had been done to it.

The other New York Chinese tradition is a calendar entry rather than a recipe: two outsider communities, neither with anywhere else to be on the twenty-fifth of December, in the same dining room, for about as long as anyone can remember.

Arrived with
Cantonese (Taishanese) migration from the 1870s; Hunanese and Sichuanese chefs after 1965.
First served here
Peng’s, Midtown, 1970s — sweetened for New York within a season.
Eat it at
Wo Hop or Hop Lee for the old Chinatown room; Hunan Slurp or Szechuan Mountain House for what came after.
The rule
On any given block, the oldest room usually wins.

In the margins

The black-and-white cookie

1902

A cakey drop cookie, baked, flipped, and iced half vanilla fondant and half chocolate. Glaser’s Bake Shop in Yorkville — German-American, 1902 to 2018 — is the New York claim; Hemstrought’s of Utica claims a half-moon that got there first. In 1994 a sitcom monologue turned it into a civics lesson about living together, and the cookie has been carrying that meaning around ever since.

The Anthora cup

1963

The blue paper coffee cup with the Greek key border and We Are Happy To Serve You was designed in 1963 by Leslie Buck, a Czech-born Holocaust survivor working for a paper company, specifically to sell to Greek-owned diners. At its peak it reportedly shipped around half a billion cups a year. It is the most widely distributed piece of design this city has ever produced and there is no name on it anywhere.

The Automat

1912–1991

Horn & Hardart put hot food behind little glass doors and took the waiter out of the transaction. At its height it fed 350,000 New Yorkers a day, and it was one of the very few public dining rooms in the city where nobody could refuse to seat you, because there was nobody to do the refusing. The last one closed on 42nd Street in 1991.

Nuyorican rice and beans

1950s

Postwar Puerto Rican migration built El Barrio and with it arroz con gandules, pernil, alcapurrias and the cuchifrito counters along 116th Street, where the fried pork parts sit under a heat lamp in a steel case and you point. La Marqueta, under the Park Avenue viaduct, was the market that supplied it. This is the food that made Puerto Rican cooking legible to the rest of the city.

1965to2000

The New Arrivals

One law reopens the city. Within a generation the menu is unrecognisable — which, if you have read this far in order, is the most predictable thing on the page.

Arriving
Dominicans ◆ Fuzhounese ◆ Guyanese and Trinidadians ◆ Jamaicans ◆ Koreans ◆ Indians ◆ Egyptians and Yemenis ◆ Mexicans
1965
The Hart–Celler Act abolishes national-origin quotas. It is, without much competition, the single most important event in the history of New York food.

199053rd & 6th

Chicken Over Rice

Three Egyptian immigrants — Mohamed Abouelenein, Ahmed Elsaka and Abdelbaset Elsayed — were running a hot dog cart at 53rd and Sixth in 1990 when Muslim cab drivers kept stopping to ask whether there was anything on it they could actually eat. There was not. So they changed the cart.

Yellow rice, chopped chicken off a flat-top, shredded lettuce, a pita folded in underneath, a white sauce nobody will give you the recipe for and a red sauce that ought to carry a warning label. It was built for people working a night shift, priced for them and portioned for them, and it spread across the city one cart at a time until it became the thing New York eats at one in the morning.

There are thousands of halal carts now. The good one is never the one with the shortest line.

Arrived with
Egyptian immigrants, post-1965; later Bangladeshi and Afghan cart operators.
First served
53rd Street & Sixth Avenue, 1990.
Eat it at
The original southeast corner after dark — or any cart with a queue of drivers rather than tourists.
The rule
White sauce generously. Red sauce in drops, and you can always add more.

1970severy borough

The Bacon, Egg and Cheese

Nobody invented the BEC, and that is the point of it. It is what happens when something like twelve thousand bodegas — built largely by Puerto Rican and then Dominican families, a great many of them now run by Yemeni ones — independently arrive at the same answer to the same question: what can a flat-top produce in ninety seconds for somebody who is already late?

A kaiser roll, split and laid face down on the grill. Bacon, an egg broken over it, a slice of American cheese. Salt, pepper, ketchup — said as one word, because in practice it is one word. Wrapped in foil and then paper, so that it steams itself soft on the way to the train.

It is the only dish on this page that every borough, every income bracket and every shift agrees on, and it costs what it costs partly because the person making it knows your face.

Arrived with
Puerto Rican and Dominican bodega networks, 1950s–70s; Yemeni ownership from the 1980s.
Eat it at
The deli nearest to you, before ten in the morning. Travelling for one defeats it.
The rule
“Bacon egg and cheese on a roll, salt pepper ketchup.” One breath. Have your money out.

In the margins

Mangú con los tres golpes

1970s–

Green plantains boiled and mashed with their own cooking water, sharp pickled red onion over the top, and the three hits: fried salami, fried cheese, fried egg. Washington Heights breakfast. Dominicans became the city’s largest immigrant group during the 1990s and the food moved with them — pernil, la bandera, and chimis off a truck outside the club at four in the morning.

Doubles and roti, Liberty Avenue

1970s–

Two soft bara rounds with curried chickpeas between them, tamarind and pepper on top, eaten out of paper while walking. Trinidadian street breakfast, sold in Richmond Hill, Queens, by a community whose ancestors were carried from India to the Caribbean as indentured labour in the 1840s. Three continents in one wrapper, handed over in ninety seconds.

Jamaican beef patty

1970s–

Turmeric-yellow shortcrust, spiced beef inside, sold hot out of a warmer in every bodega, bakery and subway newsstand in Brooklyn and the Bronx. Golden Krust, founded by the Hawthorne family from St Andrew, Jamaica, in the Bronx in 1989, turned it into a chain. The correct move is the patty inside a length of coco bread, which is a carbohydrate inside a carbohydrate and entirely defensible.

Korean Broadway, 32nd Street

1980s–

One block of Midtown, stacked four and five storeys deep with restaurants, bakeries and 24-hour karaoke, built by Korean immigrants after 1965. It is the clearest demonstration in the city that a neighbourhood does not need to be a neighbourhood — it can be a single block, running vertically, open all night.

2000tonow

The Now

A city where the cheapest slice in America and a two-hour pastry queue sit four subway stops apart, and where the newest dish on the menu had already been sold on the same corner for twenty years before anyone called it new.

Arriving
West Africans ◆ Bangladeshis ◆ Uzbeks and Central Asians ◆ Tibetans and Nepalis ◆ Mexicans, especially Pueblans ◆ Ecuadorians
2020
The pandemic closes something like a thousand restaurants and invents the outdoor dining shed. The carts and the bodegas, being already outdoors, mostly stay open.

1990scitywide 2016

The Chopped Cheese

Ground beef onto the flat-top, worked apart with the edges of two spatulas, onions in with it, American cheese laid over the top and folded through until the two stop being separate things. Into a hero roll, lettuce, tomato, mayo. It costs about what a bacon egg and cheese costs and it is a full meal.

It is generally traced to Hajji’s — Blue Sky Deli — on East 110th Street, made by Yemeni-American grill cooks for an East Harlem neighbourhood, and it was simply a sandwich there for about two decades. In 2016 a video went round, the food press announced a discovery, and Harlem pointed out, reasonably, that nothing had ever been lost. It remains the sharpest short argument this city has about who gets to decide that a thing exists.

A chopped cheese is not a Philly cheesesteak. Different meat, different cut, different sandwich, and people will correct you.

Arrived with
Yemeni-American bodega cooks in East Harlem.
Went citywide
2016, by video — about twenty years after it was first sold.
Eat it at
Blue Sky Deli (Hajji’s), First Avenue at 110th; otherwise the bodega with the busiest grill.
The rule
Ask for it chopped fine. Ketchup is optional; mayo is not.

In the margins

The dollar slice

2000s

99¢ Fresh Pizza, 2 Bros., and a price point that was never really about pizza. A slice priced so that somebody holding exactly one dollar can eat is a piece of municipal infrastructure, and it tracks rent, cheese futures and the minimum wage more honestly than any published index. It is mostly $1.50 now, which tells you the rest.

The Cronut

May 2013

Dominique Ansel laminated croissant dough, fried it, and produced a two-hour queue, a two-per-customer limit and a resale market inside a week. The pastry is good. The line was the invention, and every restaurant opening in this city since has had to decide whether it wants one.

Birria on Roosevelt Avenue

2019–

Stewed chile-red beef, the taco griddled in its own fat, a cup of consommé alongside to dip it in. It came from Jalisco by way of Tijuana and Los Angeles around 2019 and found Queens entirely ready for it. Roosevelt Avenue under the elevated 7 train is about the densest eating in North America, and birria is only the most recent thing it has absorbed without comment.

What is arriving right now

2010s–

Thieboudienne and attiéké on the Grand Concourse. Plov in Rego Park. Momos handed out of a cooler on a Jackson Heights corner. Fuchka in Kensington. In twenty years two or three of these will be on a list like this one and nobody will remember them being new, which is the only genuine tradition this city has.

Field rules

  1. Fold the slice. Always.
  2. No fork on a hot dog, a slice, or anything handed to you in paper.
  3. Order in one breath, have your money ready, step aside. The counter is a queue, not a conversation.
  4. A line of drivers is a better review than a line of tourists.
  5. Ask what came in this morning. If nobody knows, do not order the fish.
  6. If a place has one thing painted on the sign, order that thing.
  7. Nothing on this menu is improved by lettuce and tomato, with the single exception of the chopped cheese.
  8. The oldest room on the block is usually the right room on the block.

On the record

Almost every origin story in New York food is contested, and the ones that are not are usually just better marketed. Where a claim is genuinely disputed — Feltman’s first hot dog, Lombardi’s first licence, Volk’s first pastrami sandwich, the etymology of the egg cream — this page says so rather than picking a winner. The firm parts are the laws, the union charters and the business filings. “Invented by” is almost never firm.

Storefronts are the other soft spot. Places named here were open when this was written; Glaser’s Bake Shop (1902–2018), Gem Spa (1923–2020) and the last Automat (closed 1991) appear in the past tense because that is where they are. Check before you travel — this is a city that loses a hundred-year-old counter most months.
